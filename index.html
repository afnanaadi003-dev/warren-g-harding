Exhaustive analysis of the Washington Naval Treaty, civil rights in Birmingham, economic revival, and scandals

I. The Washington Naval Conference & Disarmament Diplomacy (1921–1922)

In November 1921, President Harding convened the Washington Naval Conference, inviting nine world powers to negotiate global disarmament and Pacific security. Spearheaded by Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes, the conference resulted in the landmark Five-Power Naval Treaty, establishing strict capital ship tonnage ratios (5:5:3) between the United States, Great Britain, Japan, France, and Italy.

This agreement halted a costly naval arms race, led to the scrapping of over 70 warships, and stabilized East Asian geopolitical relations for over a decade, proving to be one of the most successful international disarmament treaties in American history.

II. The Budget and Accounting Act of 1921 & Fiscal Modernization

Prior to 1921, executive departments submitted independent budget requests directly to Congress without centralized executive coordination. President Harding signed the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921, revolutionizing federal public finance by establishing:

  • Bureau of the Budget (now OMB): Empowered the President to compile and submit an annual unified federal budget.
  • General Accounting Office (GAO, now GAO): Created an independent legislative audit authority to inspect federal spending.

Working with Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon, Harding cut income tax rates, reduced government spending from $6.3 billion to $3.2 billion, and rapidly paid down the national debt.

III. Civil Rights Leadership: The 1921 Birmingham Address

On October 26, 1921, President Harding traveled to Birmingham, Alabama, to deliver a courageous address on racial equality before a segregated audience of 100,000 citizens. Harding was the first sitting U.S. President since Reconstruction to directly advocate for political, economic, and educational equality for Black Americans in the Deep South.

Harding declared: "Whether you will it or not, unless our democracy is a lie, you must stand for equality of political opportunity, for equality of educational opportunity, and for equality of economic opportunity." He also strongly supported the passage of the Dyer Anti-Lynching Bill.

IV. Administration Scandals & Objective Historical Analysis

Following his unexpected death from a heart attack in San Francisco on August 2, 1923, Harding's legacy became overshadowed by corruption scandals involving subordinates known as the "Ohio Gang." The most notable was the Teapot Dome Scandal, in which Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall secretly leased naval oil reserves in Wyoming and California to private oil companies in exchange for bribes. Fall became the first U.S. Cabinet officer convicted of a felony committed in office.

Pillars of "Return to Normalcy"

Core governance principles defined by President Warren G. Harding

1. Naval Disarmament

Led the Washington Naval Treaty to halt international arms build-ups and preserve Pacific stability.

2. Unified Federal Budget

Enacted the Budget Act of 1921 creating executive fiscal oversight and debt reduction.

3. Southern Civil Rights

Advocated political and economic equality for Black Americans and anti-lynching legislation.

4. Modern Communications

First U.S. President to utilize nationwide radio broadcasts and champion technology integration.

First Lady & Political Partner

Florence Kling Harding: Pioneer in Public Engagement

Florence Kling Harding was an energetic and influential First Lady. She actively managed newspaper circulation for the Marion Daily Star, organized front-porch press relations during the 1920 campaign, championed wounded WWI veterans, and opened the White House to public tours and press photo sessions.
